People talk about "continuity of care" as if everyone already knows the term. Many do not, and the phrase can sound like paperwork. It is a simple idea. It describes a lasting relationship between you and the physician and care team who look after your health, as opposed to a series of unrelated visits with whoever happens to be available.

This post explains what the term means, using the definitions published by the American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP), and what to look for when you are choosing a practice. It is general education only.

## A plain definition

The AAFP describes continuity of care as "the process by which the patient and their physician-led care team are cooperatively involved" in managing the patient's health. The goal it names is "safe, high quality, cost-effective medical care."

Two words in that definition matter most. The first is "process." Continuity is something that happens over time, not at one appointment. The second is "cooperatively." The patient is part of the process, not only a recipient of it.

## Sustained relationships over time

The AAFP's definition of primary care says the personal primary care physician assumes ongoing responsibility for the patient, "maintaining continuity through sustained relationships over time." The same document describes primary care as "continuous" and speaks of "developing a sustained partnership with patients."

In everyday terms, that means you have a physician who knows you, and who keeps knowing you from one year to the next. The relationship does not reset each time you come in.

:::note Worth knowing
The AAFP's primary care definition says responsibility for the patient continues across inpatient and outpatient settings.
:::

## Knowing your history and the whole person

The AAFP says continuity rests on a long-term partnership in which the physician understands the patient's history and can fold new information into a whole-person view. That is the practical heart of the idea. A physician who already knows your background does not have to start from the beginning each time.

The AAFP also says continuity helps family physicians act as patient advocates and identify potential problems early. Those are the AAFP's statements about what continuity is meant to support. They are not a promise about any one person's outcome, and nothing here is a claim about what will happen in your own care.

Continuity is closely tied to another idea in the AAFP definition. Primary care physicians are described as "trained and skilled in providing comprehensive, whole-person care." The definition says this covers undiagnosed concerns regardless of whether their origin is biological, behavioral or social, and regardless of which organ system is involved.

Put the two ideas together and you get the picture the AAFP draws. The physician sees the whole person, and the physician sees that person over time.

## It is a team, not only one person

The AAFP's continuity policy describes a "physician-led care team." Continuity depends on a team-based approach. It also says family physicians provide continuity "in all settings," both directly and through coordination with other health care professionals.

So continuity does not mean that one person does everything. It means that the people involved in your care are working together, and that someone is responsible for keeping the whole picture in view.

:::note Worth knowing
The AAFP policy on continuity of care was originally adopted in 1983 and most recently updated in October 2025.
:::

## What continuity is not

A few things are easy to confuse with continuity.

1. Seeing the same office once does not make a relationship. Continuity is about the same people over time.
2. Having records in a system is not the same as having a physician who knows you. The AAFP describes a partnership, not a file.
3. Continuity does not mean you never see anyone else. The AAFP describes a team and coordination with other professionals.

## Your part in the relationship

The AAFP's definition says the patient and the care team are "cooperatively involved." That puts some of the work on both sides. A relationship over time works better when you are able to talk openly with the physician, ask questions when something is unclear, and keep the practice informed about the care you receive elsewhere.

None of this requires medical knowledge. It means treating the practice as a long-term partner and not only as a place you visit when something comes up. The AAFP's description of a "sustained partnership" is a good way to hold the idea in mind.

## Questions to ask a practice

If you are choosing a primary care practice, these are fair questions to ask the office. They are about how the practice runs, not about your health.

1. Will I see the same physician at each visit?
2. How do I reach the physician or care team between visits?
3. How soon can I be seen when I need an appointment?
4. Who coordinates my care if I need to see another professional?
5. How does the practice keep my history in one place, and how do I see it?

The answers will tell you how much the practice is built around lasting relationships.

## Putting it together

Continuity of care is a lasting, cooperative relationship between you and a physician-led care team. The AAFP ties it to knowing a patient's history, seeing the whole person and coordinating care across settings. When you evaluate a practice, ask about who you will see, how you reach them and how your care is coordinated.
